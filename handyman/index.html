Seasonal home maintenance schedule: what to check each season, and who should do it

A home maintenance schedule is only useful if it tells you who does each job. Use this one in three columns in your head: check it yourself, hand it to a handyman, or call a licensed trade.

Spring: walk the outside after snowmelt. Look for cracked caulk around windows, lifted shingles you can see from the ground, and soil or mulch piled against siding. Test outdoor faucets slowly the first time you open them; a leak inside the wall often shows up in the first minute. Change furnace filters, test smoke and carbon monoxide alarms, and clean dryer venting as far as you can reach. Hand a handyman the ladder and repair list that comes out of that walk: resealing, minor siding and trim repair, gutter sections that have pulled loose, and screen and door fixes. Licensed trades take anything inside the systems themselves: a furnace that short cycles, an electrical fault, or a gas smell at any point in the year.

Summer: check attic ventilation on a hot day, look under every sink for slow drips, and run water in little-used bathrooms so traps stay full. Deck boards that flex, railings that move, and exterior paint that has started to peel are handyman work if caught this season, and carpenter work if the wood underneath has softened. Irrigation leaks that pool against the foundation move to the top of the list, because water against the house does more damage than most repairs on this page.

Fall: drain and shut off exterior faucets before the first hard freeze, disconnect hoses, and confirm you know where your main water shutoff is before you need it. Have heating checked before the cold arrives, seal gaps where pipes and wires enter the house, and clean gutters after leaves drop, not before. A handyman can work through the sealing, insulation touch-ups, weatherstripping, and storm door list in one visit if you hand over the list you built on your spring and summer walks.

Winter: watch for ice dams after storms, keep an eye on pipes on exterior walls during cold snaps, and open cabinet doors under sinks on the coldest nights so warm air reaches the plumbing. If a pipe freezes, shut the water off first and call a plumber; do not apply a torch or space heater to a frozen pipe yourself.

Keep the list where you will use it: copy the season you are in, date each item as you finish it, and carry what is left into the next season at the top. When a line on the list needs a second pair of hands, a ladder you do not own, or more than an hour you do not have, that line is the handyman call, and the listings on this page are the place to make it.

How to check contractor qualifications before you book

Checking a contractor or handyman takes about fifteen minutes and four questions, asked in this order, before anyone starts work in your house.

First, license, where one applies. Handyman work like patching, painting, minor carpentry, and fixture swaps is unlicensed in many places, while electrical, plumbing, and gas work needs a licensed trade in almost all of them. Ask which side of that line your job sits on, and for licensed work ask for the license number and check it with the issuing city, county, or state board before booking. A straight answer with a number you can verify is the qualification. A vague answer about being "fully qualified" is not.

Second, insurance. Ask whether the business carries liability insurance, and workers compensation if it sends employees, and ask to see a certificate or have it sent before the visit. This is a normal request in the trades, and a business that carries coverage will treat it as one. If someone is hurt or something is damaged and there is no coverage, the cost tends to land on the homeowner, which is why this question comes before price.

Third, references you can actually use. Ask for two recent jobs like yours, in your area, and what the final bill looked like against the estimate. Then call one. Ask whether they would hire the business again and whether the crew left the house as they found it. Online reviews help you spot patterns, but one real conversation about a job like yours tells you more than an average score.

Fourth, the written scope. Before work starts, get the job in writing in plain terms: what is included, what is not, what it costs, what would change the price, and how payment is staged. Never pay the full amount up front. A deposit for materials on a larger job is normal; a demand for everything in cash before work begins is a reason to stop.

If a business answers all four plainly, you have done the checking that matters, whether the job is a one-hour repair or a week of work. The listings on this page give you the name and number to start with; these four questions are how you finish the check.
