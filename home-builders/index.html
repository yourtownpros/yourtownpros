Home Builders

Building a home is the biggest purchase most people ever make, and the hardest part is not picking a builder, it is understanding what you are actually being quoted. Builders price differently, floor plans hide costs in different places, and a low headline number can leave out the lot, the permits, and half the finishes. This page lists local builders you can call directly, and the guide below explains how builder pricing works and how to compare floor plans side by side, so the quotes you collect mean the same thing. How to choose a home builder

Decor and home sites publish ranked lists of builders, and a list cannot tell you whether a firm is licensed where you live, what its contract actually covers, or whether it is taking work. That is the step this page is for.

Four checks settle most of a first call:

  1. License and insurance, verified, not quoted. Ask for the firm's license or registration number and check it with your state or county before signing anything. A real builder hands the number over without hesitation.
  2. What the price includes. Building quotes turn on allowances, the budget lines for cabinets, fixtures, flooring, and finishes. Ask what allowance each line carries and what happens when the piece you actually want costs more, because allowance overruns are where build budgets break.
  3. A payment schedule tied to stages, not dates. Money should move when work is verifiably done, not when a calendar page turns. The table below is the check to run at each stage before you pay it.
  4. The warranty, in writing. Ask what is covered for workmanship and for how long, who you call when something fails, and whether the warranty is the firm's own or a third-party policy.

If you are planning an ADU or an addition instead of a full house, the first check changes order: confirm with your city or county that the unit is allowed on your lot and what permits it needs before you spend money on design, because zoning, not construction, is where ADU projects most often stop.

So all four checks fit in that first call: the call is where price, allowances, and availability get answered. This page does not list build prices, because the price depends on the plans, the lot, and the allowances set at the first meeting; the checks are how you get a real number before you commit to anything.

How home builders price a house

There is no standard price for a new home, and any total quoted before a builder sees your lot and your plan is a guess. What you can learn is the structure, because the structure is where the surprises live. Builder pricing usually takes one of three forms:

  • A fixed price for a defined plan and spec. Common with production and semi-custom builders building in their own communities. The price is real, but only for exactly what the spec sheet lists.
  • Cost plus a fee or percentage. Common with custom builders. You pay the actual cost of labor and materials plus the builder's margin. You see every invoice, and you also carry the risk when costs move.
  • A hybrid: a fixed price on the structure with allowances for the finishes. The allowances are where these quotes go wrong (see below).

Four things decide what any quote really covers:

  1. The lot. A community price usually includes the lot. A build-on-your-lot quote almost never does, and lot prep (clearing, grading, driveway, utility runs) is a separate cost that varies enormously by site.
  2. Allowances. An allowance is a placeholder budget for something you have not chosen yet: cabinets, counters, lighting, flooring. A low allowance makes the quote look cheap and the overage arrives later, at retail, with the build already underway. Compare allowances line by line, not totals.
  3. What the spec sheet calls standard. Ceiling height, window quality, insulation, and fixtures are all priced into the base. Two builders quoting the same plan can be quoting two different houses.
  4. Exclusions. Permits, impact fees, utility hookups, landscaping, fencing, and window coverings are commonly outside the quote. Ask for the exclusion list in writing.

Price per square foot is the number everyone asks for and the one that misleads the most. Builders calculate it differently (some count only heated space, some count everything under roof), and a small home always costs more per foot than a large one because the expensive rooms, the kitchen and baths, are spread over fewer feet. Use it to spot a quote that is wildly out of line. Never use it to pick a builder.

How to compare floor plans

A floor plan is a price document in disguise. When you compare plans from different builders, check these before you compare the totals:

  • How the square footage is counted. Heated and cooled living space is the honest measure. Garages, porches, and unfinished bonus space inflate the number without adding living room.
  • Ceiling heights and roof pitch. Taller ceilings and steeper roofs cost more to build and to heat, and they are often the difference between two quotes for the same footprint.
  • The wet walls. Kitchens and baths are the expensive rooms. A plan with the kitchen, laundry, and baths sharing plumbing walls is cheaper to build than the same rooms scattered.
  • Structural options vs cosmetic options. Moving a wall, adding a bedroom, or extending a garage changes the structure and the price a lot. Counters, cabinets, and fixtures are cosmetic and easy to price later. Get structural decisions right before you sign, because changing them mid-build is the most expensive change there is.
  • What the plan assumes about the lot. A plan drawn for a flat community lot can need real money in foundation and grading work on a sloped or rural lot. Ask the builder what site assumptions the plan price carries.

Most builders will price the same plan. If you have a plan you love, getting two or three builders to quote that exact plan, with the same spec level, is the closest thing to an apples-to-apples comparison this industry offers.

Custom, semi-custom, or production: what is the difference

  • A production builder builds set plans in its own communities, at volume. Fastest and usually cheapest per home, with choices limited to its options list.
  • A semi-custom builder starts from its plan library and lets you move walls, change elevations, and pick finishes widely. The middle path on price and flexibility.
  • A custom builder builds a one-off home, from your architect's plan or a plan drawn for you, on your lot or theirs. The most control, the longest timeline, and pricing that depends on every decision you make.

None of these is the wrong answer. The right one depends on whether you own land, how much you want to decide, and how long you can wait.

How building a home works

  1. Decide how you will build: in a builder's community, or on land you own or are buying. This choice decides which builders fit.
  2. Talk to two or three builders who build that way near you. Bring your rough budget, including land, and be honest about it: a good builder designs to a number.
  3. Pick a plan, or commission one. Get the spec sheet and the allowance schedule with the quote, not after.
  4. Get the full quote in writing, with exclusions listed. Compare quotes on spec and allowances, not on the headline total.
  5. Financing and permits. Most new builds run on a construction loan that converts to a mortgage at completion. Permits are the builder's job, but the timeline is your problem too.
  6. Build and draws. The lender releases money in stages as work completes. Walk the site at each stage and raise problems the week you see them.
  7. Walkthrough and closing. You get a punch list walk before closing and a warranty after. Get the warranty terms in writing before you sign the contract, not at the end.

Questions to ask before you sign

  • Is this price fixed, cost plus, or a hybrid, and what exactly can change it?
  • What are the allowances for cabinets, counters, lighting, and flooring, and what happens when I go over?
  • What is excluded from this quote? Can I have the exclusion list in writing?
  • How is your square footage counted on this plan?
  • How many homes do you have under construction right now, and who supervises mine day to day?
  • What is the realistic timeline, and what has delayed your recent builds?
  • What warranty do you provide, in writing, and who services it after closing?
  • Can I talk to two owners whose homes you finished in the last two years?
