What an interior designer does

An interior designer plans how rooms look, feel, and function. The work usually covers space planning and furniture layout, color and finishes, lighting, window treatments, and the selection of furniture, fixtures, and materials. On remodels, a designer also produces drawings and schedules that a contractor can build and price from, and coordinates with the trades doing the work.

People hire a designer for a whole home, a kitchen or bath remodel, a single room that never came together, a condo that needs to be furnished from empty, and commercial spaces such as offices, shops, and restaurants. Some designers offer a one-time consultation and a plan you execute yourself. Others run the full project, from first sketch to the day the last piece is installed.

Interior designer or decorator: what is the difference

The words get used interchangeably, and the difference matters on bigger projects.

An interior designer works on the plan of a space: layout, lighting, built-ins, and, on remodels, the drawings and material schedules behind them. Many designers have formal training, and some states license or register interior designers for commercial work.

A decorator focuses on the surface of a finished room: furniture, fabric, color, art, and accessories, without moving walls or changing systems.

Neither is a lesser hire. A decorator is often the right call for a room refresh. A designer is the safer call when construction, permits, or built-in work is involved. Ask the person in front of you what their scope actually covers, and check that it matches your project. How hiring an interior designer works

  1. Define the project. Which rooms, what is not working, your rough budget for the whole project (furniture and labor included), and your timeline.
  2. Talk to two or three studios. Look at their past work and look for projects like yours, in size and style. Most offer an initial call or consultation.
  3. Ask how they charge before you talk about taste. Fee structures vary a lot (see the cost section below), and the right structure depends on the size of your project.
  4. Get the scope in writing. It should name the rooms, the deliverables (drawings, selections, purchasing, install supervision), what is not included, and how changes are handled.
  5. Design and selections. Expect a concept first, then specific pieces and materials. Approve in stages so surprises stay small.
  6. Purchasing and install. If the studio buys for you, ask how items are marked up and who handles delivery problems and returns.

What an interior designer costs

There is no standard price, and any total quoted before a designer sees your space is a guess. Fees are usually structured one of four ways:

  • An hourly rate. Common for consultations and smaller projects. Ask what the rate covers and get time estimates per phase.
  • A flat fee for a defined scope. Common for a single room or a furnishing plan.
  • A percentage of the project budget. Common on full-service work, where the design effort grows with the build.
  • A consultation or design-plan fee. You get a plan, selections, and sources, and you do the buying and coordination yourself.

What you pay depends on the size of the project, whether construction is involved, and your local market. A plan-only consult costs a fraction of full service on a whole home. Ask every studio you interview to state its fee basis, what each phase includes, and what triggers extra charges, in writing, before you sign.

Questions to ask before you hire

  • Is your work mostly residential, commercial, or both? Have you done projects like mine, nearby?
  • What is your fee basis, and what is not included?
  • Do you produce drawings a contractor can build from, or selections only?
  • Who handles purchasing, delivery, and returns, and how are items priced to me?
  • How many projects do you run at once, and who is my day-to-day contact?
  • How long does a project this size usually take, start to install?
  • Can I see a finished project and talk to a past client?
  • What do you need from me to start?

How to hire an interior designer

For the person hiring

YourTownPros is a free directory of local businesses, and this page lists interior designers, each with the name, phone number, and address the firm itself publishes, so you can check a firm before you call it. The category is new and growing: today it lists 3 firms, in Wilmington NC, Miami FL, and Basalt CO, and more are added as they are found and checked against the details each firm publishes. Design articles can teach you what good looks like. They cannot tell you which firm does this work, what it charges, or whether it is taking new clients. That is the step this page is for.

Three things settle most of a first call:

  1. What the first consultation covers. Ask whether the first meeting is a paid working session or a short introductory call, what you should bring (room measurements, photos, examples of rooms you like), and what you leave with. A firm that can describe its first step plainly runs the rest of the project the same way.
  2. How the firm charges. Designers generally charge one of a few ways: an hourly rate, a flat fee for a defined scope, or a fee plus a markup on furnishings bought through the firm. None of these is automatically the best deal. What matters is that the firm names its structure before work starts and puts it in writing, so ask for exactly that.
  3. What the scope includes and excludes. Ask who buys the furniture, who receives deliveries, who hires the painter or the carpenter, and what happens if a piece arrives damaged. Most bad design experiences are scope misunderstandings, not bad taste.
