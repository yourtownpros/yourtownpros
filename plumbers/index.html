Before you call a plumber: a quick check

Before you call, take a minute to check the pro you are about to hire.

Compare the details. The phone number and hours on the listing here should match the map profile and the business's own site. If they disagree, ask on the call which ones are current.

Read recent reviews for jobs like yours. Look for clear explanations, a price that matched the quote, and problems handled well if something went wrong.

Ask two plain questions before work starts: will you give a written estimate, and will you call before doing anything extra? The answers tell you a lot about how the business runs.

That short check works whether the business has a storefront or travels to you from a truck or home base. Businesses that travel to customers have no shop sign to check against, so their listing details matter even more.

Run a business in this trade?

If you run a plumbing business, keep your name, phone number, and hours the same everywhere you are listed: your Google Business Profile, the map apps, and your listing here. If you travel to customers instead of working from a storefront, you can set the areas you serve in your Google Business Profile instead of publishing a street address.

Where a business appears in Google Maps depends on several factors together, including how close it is to the person searching, its category, its reviews, and how well known it is overall. Keeping your profile accurate and consistent is one factor among several. It helps customers trust what they find, but it does not guarantee any ranking.

Hiring a plumber for a remodel: how to vet one before the walls open

For the person hiring: YourTownPros is a free directory of local businesses, and this guide sits with our plumber listings, 838 plumbers organized by city and state. A remodel changes the hiring stakes. A plumber who is fine for a dripping faucet may not be the right fit for moving drain lines, and the mistakes are expensive because they hide behind new tile and drywall. Before you hire anyone for remodel work, work through these checks.

Check the license and insurance first. Most states and many cities license plumbers, and remodel work that moves supply or drain lines usually needs a permit. Ask for the license number, then look it up with the issuing board. Ask for proof of liability insurance as well. A company that does this work every week will hand both over without hesitation.

Ask about remodel experience specifically. How many bathroom or kitchen remodels have they plumbed in the last year? Will they coordinate the rough-in schedule with your general contractor, or do you need to line the dates up yourself? Rough-in happens before walls close, so a plumber who misses that window delays every trade after them.

Get the estimate in writing, itemized. It should name the fixtures or fixture allowances, the pipe work included, permit fees, and what counts as a change order. Two estimates that look far apart usually differ in what they leave out, not in what they charge.

Read reviews for the pattern, not the average. Look for comments about showing up on schedule, communicating during the job, and how problems were handled. A company with detailed reviews about remodel work tells you more than a perfect score built on drain calls.

Finally, ask who actually does the work. Some companies send their own crews; others subcontract. Either can be fine. What matters is that one named person is accountable for the plumbing passing inspection.

Remodeling a bathroom or kitchen: the plumbing order of work

YourTownPros is a free directory of local businesses, and this guide sits with our plumber listings, organized by city and state. If you only need a number, use the plumber listings for your city on this page and skip the guide. A remodel is where plumbing stops being a repair and becomes a sequence. The fixtures are the part everyone shops for first, but the order the work happens in decides whether the project runs smoothly, and the plumber is in that sequence earlier than most homeowners expect.

Start with the bathroom, because it concentrates the most plumbing in the smallest room. Before any tile, tub, or vanity is ordered, decide what stays where it is. A toilet, tub, or shower that keeps its current position usually keeps its drain and supply lines, and the plumbing work stays short. Moving any of them means opening the floor or the wall to reroute pipe, which adds days and a permit in most places, and it has to happen before anything decorative goes in. The practical checks, in order:

  1. Walk the room with the plumber before demolition and mark which fixtures move and which stay. Get that in writing, because the estimate follows from it.
  2. Ask whether the existing shut-off valves hold. Remodels are the cheapest moment to replace valves that have not been touched in years, while the water is already off and the walls are already open.
  3. Settle the fixture list before rough-in. The rough-in measurements follow the actual tub, valve, and toilet you chose, not a placeholder, and changing a valve after rough-in can mean reopening finished work.
  4. Ask who schedules the rough-in inspection and what has to be true before the walls close. Nothing covers the plumbing until it passes.

A kitchen renovation runs on the same logic with a longer timeline. The usual order is demolition, then rough plumbing and electrical, then inspection, then walls, cabinets, counters, and finally fixture hookup. The sink position is the kitchen version of the bathroom rule: keeping it on the same wall is a short plumbing job, moving it across the room is not. If the renovation adds a dishwasher, a pot filler, or a refrigerator water line where none existed, say so at the first plumbing visit, not after the cabinets are set. A week-by-week plan that names which trade is in the house each week, and what each one needs finished before it arrives, prevents the most common renovation delay: a trade showing up to work that depends on a visit nobody scheduled.

Separate from any remodel, one checkup belongs in every season, and it is the one to run before guests arrive or a holiday fills the house. Run the rarely used bathroom: flush it, run its taps, and look under its sinks for slow drips that have had months to soak a cabinet floor. Test the water heater's temperature setting and look at its base for rust or pooling. Find the main shut-off valve and make sure it turns; the time to learn it is stuck is not during a leak. These checks take an afternoon at most, and every one of them is cheaper to fix on a quiet week than on a holiday morning.
